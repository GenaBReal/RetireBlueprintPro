How much can we safely spend in retirement? A real example

Michael and Linda are 62 with $550,000 saved. Here's exactly how much their plan says they can spend, year by year, and why the 4% rule gets it wrong.

By Gena Philpott, founder of RetireBlueprint Pro · Updated October 2026

The short answer: After covering their must-pay bills of $73,500 a year (living costs, healthcare and the mortgage), Michael and Linda's plan lets them spend an extra $22,497 a year in their early, active years, $19,563 in their middle years and $17,606 later on. With that spending, and assuming steady average returns, the plan lasts until Linda is 92 and still leaves about $100,000. (See the crash test for what happens when markets don't cooperate.)

Why the 4% rule gives the wrong answer

The popular 4% rule says you can spend 4% of your savings each year. For Michael and Linda that's about $22,000 a year. It sounds close to their real answer, but it gets there by accident and misses most of what matters:

  • It ignores Social Security and pensions. From 2031, their Social Security plus Linda's pension cover a large share of their bills. The planner shows their retirement income replacing about 89% of their working income.
  • It ignores that bills change. Their mortgage disappears in 2034, which frees up $13,800 a year.
  • It assumes you spend the same amount forever. Most people spend more in their 60s (travel, projects) and less in their 80s.

A real plan looks at every year separately: what comes in, what has to go out, and what's left to enjoy.

What happens to their money over time

  • 2026: Salaries still cover everything, so they don't touch savings.
  • 2027: Their first withdrawal from savings. Their savings peak at about $661,000 this year.
  • 2031: Both Social Security checks start, which takes pressure off their savings.
  • 2034: The mortgage is paid off.
  • 2039: Required minimum distributions (RMDs) from their 401(k) and 403(b) begin at age 75.
  • 2052–2056: The plan assumes Michael lives to 88 and Linda to 92. It's built to keep Linda fully funded as a survivor, and it ends with about $100,000.

Over the whole plan, they pay an estimated $110,000 in federal and state taxes, including capital gains. That's one more reason a year-by-year plan beats a single percentage.

The catch: markets don't move in straight lines

These numbers assume steady average returns. Real markets jump around, and a bad year right after you retire hurts the most. We tested exactly that for Michael and Linda in what happens if the market crashes the year you retire.

Quick answers

How much can a couple with $550,000 spend in retirement?

It depends on their Social Security, pensions, bills and goals. For our sample couple Michael and Linda, both 62, the plan allows an extra $22,497 a year in their early retirement years on top of $73,500 of must-pay bills, while still leaving $100,000 at the end, assuming steady average returns.

Is the 4% rule a good guide?

It's a rough starting point, but it ignores Social Security, pensions, a mortgage that ends, and spending that changes with age. A year-by-year plan gives a much more accurate number.

Does retirement spending stay the same every year?

Usually not. Many retirees spend more in their active early years and less later. Michael and Linda's plan uses three phases that step down over time.
