Roth conversions before RMDs: when they help and when they don't

You'll hear that everyone should do Roth conversions in early retirement. For our sample couple, the planner found they wouldn't save anything. Here's why, and how to tell which camp you're in.

By Gena Philpott, founder of RetireBlueprint Pro · Updated October 2026

The short answer: A Roth conversion only saves money if the tax you pay now is lower than the tax you'd pay later. Michael and Linda have a 12-year window to convert (2027 to 2038), but at their moderate savings level and their assumed future tax rate, their planner shows $0 in lifetime savings from converting. For them, it's an optional tool, not a must-do.

What a Roth conversion is

You move money from a pre-tax account, like a traditional 401(k), 403(b) or IRA, into a Roth IRA, and you pay income tax on the amount you move this year. After that it grows tax-free, and there are no required withdrawals from the Roth during your lifetime. You're choosing to pay tax now instead of later.

The conversion window

The best time to convert is usually after your paychecks stop but before required minimum distributions (RMDs) start, when your taxable income is lowest. Under current law, RMDs start at 73, or at 75 for people born in 1960 or later. Michael and Linda were born in 1964, so theirs start at 75.

Why converting doesn't pay off for them

  • Their RMDs will be moderate. With $475,000 in pre-tax savings that they're also spending from, required withdrawals at 75 aren't large enough to push them into a much higher tax bracket.
  • Their tax rates now and later look similar. A conversion is taxed at the bracket it lands in, and for them that bracket isn't expected to be much higher later, so paying tax earlier saves little.
  • Converting has its own costs. Taxes paid on a conversion have to come from somewhere, and that money stops growing for them.

When conversions usually do help

  • Large pre-tax balances, where RMDs would push you into higher brackets later.
  • A widow or widower who will file single. The survivor's income usually drops less than their tax brackets do, which shrink by about half.
  • Big gaps between retiring and Social Security, with years of very low income to "fill up" a low bracket.
  • Leaving money to children who may be in a higher bracket than you, since inherited Roth money comes out tax-free.

Traps to watch for

  • Medicare IRMAA: conversion income can raise your Medicare Part B and D premiums two years later.
  • Health insurance before 65: a conversion counts as income for Marketplace premium help, so it can raise what you pay for coverage.
  • Social Security taxes: extra income can make more of your Social Security taxable.
  • The 5-year rule: each conversion has its own 5-year clock before the converted amount can be withdrawn penalty-free if you're under 59½.

Quick answers

Should I do Roth conversions in early retirement?

Only if the tax you pay now is lower than what you'd pay later. For our sample couple with $475,000 in pre-tax savings and an 11.4% effective tax rate, the planner found no lifetime savings. Larger balances or a future single-filing survivor often change the answer.

When do RMDs start?

Under current law, required minimum distributions start at 73, or at 75 for people born in 1960 or later. Check the IRS RMD page for your situation.

Can a Roth conversion raise my Medicare premiums?

Yes. Medicare's IRMAA surcharge is based on your income from two years earlier, and conversion income counts.
